This blog has been pretty quiet since its inception. It's often difficult for me to find the time to sit down and polish a blog post over multiple sessions until I feel that it is of a high enough quality for me to release to the world. This isn't to say that I assume there are ravenous readers here, but I do link this site on my resume, and in job applications asking for a personal site, so there is always the possibility that some recruiter glanced over an article. And for that recruiter, I want that glance to look good!

Unfortunately, this has lead me to a sort of paralysis. I have ideas that I want to write about, but I'm unsure about the level of rigor that I should use. Does every sentence need a link or an entry in a "Works Cited" section? Or is it ok to just push out a Twitter-style microblog riddled with undefended opinions for the sake of actually shipping some content to this mostly empty space?

I see this problem as a tradeoff between velocity and rigor, and so I've sought to try to define some rules for myself moving forward. My ideal end state is that these rules will help me reach two goals:
1. Actually write blog posts
2. Represent what I know honestly, including its limits

I'm going to talk a little about how I developed the rules first, and then I'll show off the rules themselves at the end.
## Shaping the rules

The first part of my research for these rules was to ask: "What do other prolific writers or blog posters have to say about writing good blog posts?"[^1]

[Paul Graham](https://paulgraham.com/useful.html) says that an essay should be "Useful", and defines the four dimensions of Importance, Novelty, Correctness, and Strength.

> Useful writing tells people something true and important that they didn't already know, and tells them as unequivocally as possible.

He discusses only writing what you're sure about, and either adding qualifiers to, or refraining from offering any opinions on, what you're less sure about. He also notes that essays are not subject to any time constraints, and can be edited or reworked for as long as needed.

I really like this framework, but I think it's more suited to deep, possibly opinionated essays, rather than my blog posts. I imagine my blog as many heterogeneous forms of content, some small, with possible foundations to build on later, or maybe a series that develops as I learn more. Some long and well thought out, persuasive and useful, or maybe technical odysseys of ongoing projects. I think that the permission to rework or edit a smaller blog post forever directly contributes to my shipping paralysis.

[Julia Evans](https://jvns.ca/blog/2017/03/20/blogging-principles/) has notes on writing good blog posts, and the ones I've cherry-picked as relevant to me, echo the essay notes of Paul, but to a less formal degree. 

She discusses being honest about what you know, trying not to write anything too wrong, and sticking to writing about your own experience, as opposed to giving advice. It's alright to admin uncertainty, and accept that some tiny mistakes will survive. What I like about this is that she allows herself to publish without absolute certainty.

A particularly appealing idea I found was that of ["working with the garage door up"](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zCMhncA1iSE74MKKYQS5PBZ). 

[Austin Kleon](https://austinkleon.com/2015/06/14/to-be-a-teacher-and-remain-a-student/)[^3] quotes a C.S Lewis passage about learning out in the open, and argues that writing about things one is actively learning lets others look over your shoulder as you try and figure things out. [Shawn Wang](https://swyx.io/learn-in-public) similarly writes about learning in public. He talks about creating content that you wish you had found when you were learning, and that you should write content for a past you, while your future you benefits. [Maggie Appleton](https://maggieappleton.com/garden-history) writes about the broader concept of a Digital Garden[^4], which is of a system for organizing evolving notes and ideas.

I like the idea of "working with the garage door up" a lot, because there is [documented evidence](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0361476X13000209?via%3Dihub) that attempting to explain a concept while learning it, can help to improve learning for the student performing the explanation. I see permission to publish half-baked ideas, with an understanding between the writer and the reader that these ideas might not be fully fleshed out, as a form of explaining concepts as a student. My hope is that publishing unfinished work sooner will also lead to a greater number of posts and writing "reps" or practice and then improvement.

The last area I pulled from was blogs that I follow or read semi-regularly. Why are these blogs interesting to me?

[Bryan Cantrill](https://bcantrill.dtrace.org/) has decades of industry experience and deep-seated convictions about the way things ought to be, and his writing make me thing about best practices for my own work, and my values as a person.

[Eli Bendersky](https://eli.thegreenplace.net/) blogs about all sorts of interesting content from programming languages and their features, to math notes, to book reviews, which inspires me to go out, read new books, do more projects, and generally learn more things.

[Mitchell Hashimoto](https://mitchellh.com/writing) is a very successful programmer and founder who writes about niche technical problems he experiences in his open source projects, and his startup journey. These make me dream about one-day starting my own business and/or contributing to a prolific open-source project.

I think the common themes here are inspiration about things I care about, and though-provocation. Reading Eli's "[Revisting: 'Let's Build a Compiler'](https://eli.thegreenplace.net/2025/revisiting-lets-build-a-compiler/)" directly influenced me to buy Robert Nystrom's Crafting Interpreters and dip my toes into compilers. I want to capture this spirit in my blog.
## The Rules

1. Posts should be reasonable in length. Five minutes of reading max. If a post is a longer essay, this should be called out at the beginning to respect the reader's time.
2. Posts should have a clear message, demonstration, or conclusion.
3. The scope and confidence of a claim should match the evidence behind it. Qualifiers should be used to narrow a claim when necessary.
	1. When developing claims, identify exactly what happened, and then write it.
	2. Talk about my own experience, rather than offering advice from up-on-high.
4. Revise while changes improve what I am saying or how clearly I say it, but there is no need to resolve every uncertainty before publication.
5. Write about things that I find interesting, not things that I think others will find interesting.

Thanks for Reading!
